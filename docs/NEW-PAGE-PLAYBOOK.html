A briefing from the ICJIA web team for new colleagues and managers. September 2026.

I have a new page. How do I get people to see it?

Not by putting it on the front page. Publishing makes a page findable. Outreach makes it found. It is the first question most new colleagues ask, and a good one. Here is what the site's own numbers say, and what to ask for instead.

Two kinds of discovery

The website can only do one of them. Passive discovery is built into the site and runs on its own. Active discovery is done by people, on purpose, on a schedule. A page that only gets the first one is the homicide page above.

Passive

The site waits for someone who is already looking.

  • Site search. The site's search index is regenerated every time the site is rebuilt, so a page is searchable from the next rebuild on. Pages built outside the content system, like the homicide dashboard, are listed once by the developer and then included in every rebuild.
  • Search engines. Every rebuild also publishes a sitemap that tells Google and Bing what exists. Indexing takes days to weeks, and ranking depends heavily on how many other pages link to ours.
  • Menus and context bars. A map of the agency for people who already know what they want.
  • Homepage slots. News, funding, meetings, and jobs, filled automatically from the content system.

Permanent, free, and compounding. Also slow, small, and blind to anyone who was not already searching.

Active

We go to the people who should see it.

  • CJ Dispatch email. 4,000+ subscribers who chose to hear from us. Fastest and largest single push we have.
  • Social posts. LinkedIn for practitioners, X for press, Facebook for the public. Reaches people who never search for us.
  • Partner links. Links from idoc.illinois.gov alone sent 1,800 visits last year. ISP, ILETSB, and county partners can do the same for a page about their data.
  • A Research Hub article. A short findings piece linking to the page, published in the channel that already carries half our readership.
  • Press. A data release with a state mandate behind it is a story.

Fast and measurable. Owned by Communications, not by the website.

Active feeds passive. Every email, post, and partner link creates a visit today and an inbound link that raises the page's search ranking for years. Outreach is how a page earns the passive traffic that later looks free.

Why "just put it on the front page" does not work

It is the most common request from new colleagues, and it comes from a reasonable instinct: the front page must be where everyone looks. The numbers say otherwise, and the site's own history says why we stopped doing it.

A link almost nobody sees

Only one visit in five starts on the homepage, and the homepage already carries dozens of links. A new one competes with all of them for a reader who, three times out of five, leaves after a single page.

A menu is a map, not a billboard

The menu describes how the agency is organized so that people who know what they want can get there. Every item added for visibility makes every other item harder to find, and a menu has never told anyone about something they did not know existed.

Exceptions become the rule

One custom box on the front page is a precedent. The next request cannot be refused, and within a year the front page is the carnival again. Fixed slots are the only rule that survives the next manager.

The right way onto the front page: publish a news post about the page. It appears in the News slot as soon as it is published, stays while it is recent, and steps aside for the next item. That is the front page working as designed, and it takes twenty minutes.

The launch playbook

The order matters: the news post and the email create the first wave, social and partner links widen it, and the Hub article makes it last. Every step uses a channel that already exists.

  1. 1
    Before launch
    Write the announcement once, in three lengths

    A news post, an email item, and three social posts, each saying what the page answers and for whom. Build one tagged link per channel (see below). Send the tagged links to Communications with the copy.

  2. 2
    Launch day
    Publish the news post and send CJ Dispatch

    The post fills the homepage News slot and the news feed. The email reaches 4,000 subscribers within the hour. Expect the largest single day of traffic the page will ever have.

  3. 3
    Week 1
    Post on LinkedIn, X, and Facebook, and ask partners to link

    Three posts from three angles: the mandate, the headline finding, the county view. Ask the agencies whose data it is to link from their own sites. One partner link is worth more than a year of homepage placement.

  4. 4
    Weeks 2 to 6
    Publish a Research Hub article about what the data shows

    The Hub carries 47% of the site's page views, and its articles keep drawing readers for years. Google and AI assistants can quote an article; they cannot read a dashboard. Link the article to the page and the page to the article.

  5. 5
    Every quarter
    Repeat with each data release

    A new reporting period is a new announcement. Refresh the article's numbers, send the email, post again. Recurring content is what turns a page into a destination.

  6. 6
    Always
    Tag every link, and read the numbers 30 days later

    Tagged links are the only way to know which of the steps above worked. The readout takes ten minutes and decides what to do more of next time.

Tagged links: how we would know what worked

A UTM tag is a short label on the end of a link that names where the link was posted. The page ignores it. Our analytics reads it and credits the visit to that channel. That is the entire technology, and it is free.

icjia.illinois.gov/homicide
?utm_source=cjdispatch
&utm_medium=email
&utm_campaign=homicide-sept2026
  • utm_sourceWhere it was posted: cjdispatch, linkedin, facebook, x, or a partner such as idoc.
  • utm_mediumWhat kind of channel: email, social, referral.
  • utm_campaignWhich release: homicide-sept2026, then homicide-dec2026 for the next quarter.

The rules are short. Tags go on links distributed outside the site: emails, posts, partner pages, printed QR codes. Never on the site's own internal links, which would corrupt the count. Use the same spelling every time so the reports add up.

With tags in place, the 30-day readout answers the questions a manager actually asks. How many visits did the email produce? Did LinkedIn or Facebook do better? Did the partner link matter? Should we send again next quarter? Without tags, every one of those answers is a guess.

Communications does not tag its links. That is a mistake.

99.9%

of last year's 144,600 visits carried no tag we created. Ninety carried an email tag, and five a social tag, nearly all from another agency's mailing system.

  1. Our biggest channel is a black box. 48% of visits arrive as "Direct", where email, Teams, and app clicks land with their origin stripped. A tagged link keeps its credit because the label travels inside the link itself.
  2. Untagged campaigns cannot be improved. Every send and post so far is unmeasured, so nothing can be learned from it, and no one can show that the work produced anything.
  3. It costs nothing. A tag takes a minute to add and works in every channel we use. There is no budget, tool, or approval involved.

What to ask for instead

Each item below is a channel that already exists and a person who already owns it. Together they take a few hours spread over a month. None of them changes the website.

"Can we put a link on the front page?"

The request is really "I want people to see this." These are the things that make people see it.

  • A news post on launch day. It puts the page in the homepage News slot automatically.
  • A CJ Dispatch email to the 4,000 people who asked to hear from us.
  • Three social posts over the first week, LinkedIn first.
  • Partner links from the agencies whose data the page presents.
  • A Research Hub article about what the data shows, linking to the page.
  • A tagged link for every channel, and a readout 30 days later.

Other good questions

Colleagues who joined after the site was built ask these often. Short answers here; the web team is glad to give longer ones.

Can we change the homepage or the menu for this?
Changes to the site's structure go through agency review and ship in scheduled releases. They are slow by design, and they are not what makes a page seen. Nothing in the playbook above changes the website.
Why not change the Research Hub while we are at it?
A findings article in the Hub is exactly right, and it needs no change to the Hub. A rebuilt Research Hub is complete and waiting on agency approval to launch. Until then, the current Hub keeps carrying half the site's readership, which is reason enough to publish there now.
The page could be better. Should we fix it first?
Improvements to a page ship in small releases as they are approved, and the site is audited against automated accessibility rules. Outreach does not need to wait for a better page. A page nobody visits is not improved by polishing.
Who does what?
The page owner writes the announcement and asks partners to link. The web team publishes the page and the news post and supplies the tagged links. Communications sends the email and posts to social. The web team pulls the numbers 30 days later.
