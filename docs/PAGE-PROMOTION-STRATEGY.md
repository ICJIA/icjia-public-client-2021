# Publishing Isn't Promotion

**ICJIA Web Team · Briefing · August 26, 2026 · For discussion**

The Homicide Reporting page is live and working. Now it needs an audience — and our own analytics say that audience will not come from passive placement, whether that's a menu link or a news item on the homepage. It comes when we push: the **CJ Dispatch** list, **social channels**, and a **Research Hub article**. Here is the evidence, and a repeatable playbook for this page and every state-mandated page that follows it.

**The numbers at a glance** *(ICJIA's own Plausible analytics, trailing 12 months)*

| | |
|---:|:---|
| **9** | visitors to /homicide in its first days, unannounced — the passive baseline |
| **4 in 5** | visits start on an interior page — most visitors never see the homepage |
| **48%** | of all pageviews happen in the Research Hub |
| **4,000+** | CJ Dispatch subscribers — opted-in criminal-justice professionals |
| **~2%** | of traffic is *credited* to social — a floor, not the truth: untagged app & email clicks are filed under "Direct" |

## 1. How people actually find icjia.illinois.gov

Over the last 12 months the site drew **61,100 visitors** across **146,300 visits** and **463,700 pageviews**. About **90%** of those visits arrived through a search engine or as "Direct" — the bucket where analytics also files every click whose origin it cannot see (more on that below). People land on the exact page that answers their question, read it, and leave (63% bounce rate). Only 1 visit in 5 even begins at the homepage. Almost nobody arrives at the front door and browses the menus looking for something new.

And where they land is overwhelmingly one place: the **Research Hub carries 48% of all pageviews**, and seven of the site's ten most-visited pages are Hub articles.

One new development worth naming: **AI assistants are now our #3 referrer.** ChatGPT sent ~2,500 visitors last year — more than Bing. Search engines and AI assistants have one thing in common: they can only recommend pages whose *text* they can read.

## 2. What a menu link (alone) can deliver

A menu item serves people who are already on the site for another reason. That is *findability* — necessary, cheap, and worth doing on day one. But it creates no new audience. What navigation-only reach looks like, in average visitors per day over the last 12 months:

| Page | Visitors/day |
|:---|---:|
| Top Research Hub article (promoted + search-indexed) | **16** |
| Typical menu-reached page (e.g. /about/dicra) | **3** |
| Best single news post of the year (homepage news item) | **≈2** |

The Homicide Reporting page has a second, structural problem: its centerpiece is an embedded Tableau dashboard. To Google — and to ChatGPT — an embedded dashboard is nearly invisible. The page carries very little indexable text, so organic search will not build this audience on its own, either. Left passive, /homicide stays at tens of visitors a month.

There *is* a smart version of on-site promotion — it just isn't the menu. Since visitors enter through articles, promote where they land: the ten most-read Hub articles alone logged **≈42,000 readers** last year. A "New: Illinois Homicide Clearance Dashboard" callout inside the most-read related articles puts the page in front of the site's real front door. Worth doing — but note its limit: on-site placement can only *convert* traffic we already have. It cannot *create* traffic. Creating it is what the push channels are for.

## 3. What deliberate outreach delivers

**CJ Dispatch (Constant Contact) — highest yield, first 48 hours.** 4,000+ subscribers who chose to hear from us. At a deliberately conservative 30% open / 3% click — at or below published government-sector benchmarks (30–47% open, 2–4% click) — one send produces **≈1,200 reads** and **≈120 visits** within two days. That is roughly **three months of the passive baseline in 48 hours**, delivered to the equivalent of 70% of a typical month's entire site audience. And if past Dispatch sends drove clicks, our analytics never saw them: untagged email lands in "Direct" too.

**Social (LinkedIn, X, Facebook) — compounding, over weeks.** Social's *credited* share is ~2% of traffic — ≈1,500 visitors last year, and it has sat in that range since tracking began in 2021 (Facebook runs a steady 50–150 visitors/month: ambient shares, no campaign spikes). Credited is a floor — social apps routinely strip the referrer — but the pattern is clear: we rarely push. The practitioner audience lives on LinkedIn; posts get re-shared by partner agencies and picked up by local press. With sustained posting, a realistic year-one outcome is social growing from ~2% to 5–10% of traffic — and its unique value is reach: it is the one channel that finds people who never search for us.

**Research Hub article — durable, over quarters.** A short findings piece — what the clearance data actually shows — published in the channel that already carries 48% of our pageviews, linking to the dashboard. It also creates the crawlable narrative that search engines and AI assistants can cite, which the embedded dashboard cannot provide. Hub articles compound: our top article still drew 5,900 visitors last year. Refresh the article with each quarterly data release.

> **Why "2% from social" is a floor — what "Direct" is hiding**
>
> - **48% of all visits are "Direct,"** and 7 in 10 of them begin on deep interior URLs — article addresses nobody types by hand. Those are followed links whose origin was stripped before our analytics could see it.
> - **Direct traffic skews desktop (77%),** which points at email clients, Teams, and shared documents as the biggest hidden pipe — exactly where CJ Dispatch clicks would land. The fragments that do self-identify (Teams, Word Online, Gmail, Adobe) prove each of these pipes is active.
> - **Social apps only partially self-report:** we see their link-shims (m./l./lm.facebook.com, t.co, l.instagram.com) when a referrer survives — and nothing when it doesn't. A UTM-tagged link is immune, because the credit rides in the URL itself.

**Two supporting moves round it out.** A homepage **news item** is worth posting, but single news posts historically top out around 600 visitors a year — it's the garnish, not the meal. And **partner links** matter: IDOC's website alone sent us 1,600 visitors last year; sister agencies should be asked to link the dashboard from their statistics pages.

## 4. The playbook — launch sequence for any state-mandated page

1. **Day 1 — Findability floor.** Menu link, internal site search, sitemap. Do it — just don't mistake it for promotion.
2. **Week 1 — Research Hub article.** Three or four key findings in plain language, a chart or two, prominent link to the live page.
3. **Week 1 — CJ Dispatch feature.** Lead item or dedicated send, linking to the article and the page.
4. **Weeks 1–4 — Social push.** Launch post on LinkedIn, X, and Facebook, then one stat-of-the-week per week for a month.
5. **Week 1 — Homepage news item.** Posted the same day as the Dispatch send, so homepage visitors see a consistent story.
6. **Weeks 1–2 — In-article callouts.** Add a short "New: Illinois Homicide Clearance Dashboard" promo to the most-read related Hub articles — the site's real front door (~42,000 readers/year across the top ten).
7. **Week 2 — Partner links.** Ask IDOC, ISP, ILETSB, and other sister agencies to link the page from their data/statistics sections.
8. **Always — Tag every link** (utm_source, utm_campaign). 98% of our visits carry no campaign tag, so email and social wins hide inside "Direct." A tagged link survives app and email referrer-stripping because the credit travels in the URL itself — the few tagged links that reach us today (e.g., another agency's govdelivery emails) attribute perfectly. Tagging turns the next launch into evidence.
9. **Weeks 2 & 6 — Report actuals.** A one-screen Plausible readout to managers: visits by channel, downloads, dashboard views. Closes the loop and builds the case for the next launch.

## 5. UTM tags: how we prove what worked

A UTM tag is a short label added to the end of a link's address that names where the link was posted. The page ignores it; our analytics reads it. This is the entire technology:

```
icjia.illinois.gov/homicide?utm_source=cjdispatch&utm_medium=email&utm_campaign=homicide-sept2026
```

Why it matters here: a tagged link attributes correctly *no matter what* — even when a social app or email client strips the referrer, because the credit travels inside the URL itself. It costs nothing, requires no code changes to the site, and Constant Contact can append tags automatically to every link in a send. With tags in place, the week-two report stops being "traffic went up" and becomes "the Dispatch send drove 143 visits, LinkedIn 51, the news item 12" — per channel, per campaign, per quarter.

The convention: **utm_source** names the channel (cjdispatch, linkedin, facebook, x, partner-idoc), **utm_medium** the type (email, social, referral), **utm_campaign** the release (homicide-sept2026). Tags go on links we distribute *outside* the site — never on the site's own internal links, which would corrupt the numbers.

## The bottom line

**A menu item files the page. Outreach announces it.** Do both — but only one creates traffic.

Passive-only, /homicide plateaus at tens of visitors a month. With the playbook, it gets hundreds of qualified visitors in week one, durable search and AI discovery through the Hub article, and a measured result we can show — a sequence we can repeat, on schedule, for every page the state asks us to stand up.

Ranked honestly: the **Dispatch send** is the surest immediate win; the **Hub article** is the one that keeps paying; **social** multiplies both and is the only channel that reaches people who never search for us. No single channel is "the key" — the sequence is.
